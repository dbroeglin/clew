# Average speed

<!-- clew:nav -->
Course: [[courses/mechanics/course|Mechanics of everyday motion]] · Chapter: [[courses/mechanics/chapters/motion|Describing motion]]
Previous: none · Next: [[courses/mechanics/sections/speed-from-a-graph|Speed from a distance-time graph]]
Originals: [[courses/mechanics/sources/mechanics-notes.pdf#page=1|mechanics-notes.pdf, page 1]]
<!-- /clew:nav -->

Average speed is a ratio: the distance covered divided by the time taken. It
teaches [[concepts/physics-average-speed|average speed]] and assumes you can
already read a [[concepts/math-ratios|ratio]].

Write $\Delta s$ for the distance covered and $\Delta t$ for the time it took.
Then the average speed is

$$
\bar v = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}.
$$

A journey of $\Delta s = 150\ \mathrm{km}$ taking $\Delta t = 2\ \mathrm{h}$ has
an average speed of $75\ \mathrm{km/h}$.

## What the average leaves out

The average says nothing about how the speed varied. The same
$75\ \mathrm{km/h}$ average describes a steady cruise and a journey that
alternated between a standstill and $150\ \mathrm{km/h}$.

Because it is a ratio, scaling both quantities together leaves it unchanged:
$300\ \mathrm{km}$ in $4\ \mathrm{h}$ is still $75\ \mathrm{km/h}$. That is the
property carried over from ratios, not a separate fact about motion.

## Units follow the division

The unit of an average speed is the unit of distance divided by the unit of
time. Dividing kilometres by hours gives $\mathrm{km/h}$; dividing metres by
seconds gives $\mathrm{m/s}$. A speed quoted without its unit is not a speed.
